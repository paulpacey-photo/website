The ugly: the authenticity contradiction

Every school I’ve worked with says some version of the same thing: we value authenticity. Our community is real. Come and see for yourself. So here’s the question every school should ask before publishing AI imagery: how can you say you value authenticity, then present your school with images that aren’t real?

  • Invented students. On LinkedIn, I followed a long discussion about an AI tool marketed to schools that “anonymizes” students by replacing their faces with generated ones. Commenters were appalled that the results looked like idealized “poster children.” Every shoot involves casting; we photograph the students who shine in front of a camera, and there’s nothing wrong with that. But choosing real students who photograph well is very different from replacing them with students who don’t exist. The first shows your community at its best. The second shows a community you don’t have.
  • The label undermines the proof. Parents look at your photos for evidence of what life at your school is really like. An “AI-generated” label tells them that what they’re looking at isn’t evidence.
  • Everything starts to look the same. AI learns from what already exists, so it drifts toward the average. Schools already struggle to stand apart; generated imagery makes it harder.
  • The safeguarding climate. Parents are hearing about school photos being taken from websites and manipulated with AI (TechRound). In that climate, “we used AI on your child’s photo” is a hard sentence to say, however good your intentions.

Keeping the magic

The answer isn’t to abandon AI. It’s to move it.

Behind the camera, everything in “the good” list above is still open to you. Those uses save time, protect students and mislead no one.

In front of the camera, one of the most exciting opportunities sits where the rules are kindest: content that is obviously not real. Picture a Primary student learning about the Stone Age who drifts into a daydream and finds herself in a prehistoric cave. Or a lesson on the planets that ends with a student standing on Mars. Nobody thinks it actually happened, so it isn’t pretending to be real. What it shows is true: what a great lesson does to a child’s imagination. And it would cut straight through the sea of identical corridor-and-classroom content most schools publish.

To do it well:

  • Keep the child real; let AI build the world. Film or photograph the real student and the real wonder, then place them in an AI-generated environment. The child’s face and emotion are never synthesized. You’ll still need parental consent for the use, and should check with your legal team whether a label applies, but you’re on far firmer ground.
  • Bookend it with reality. If creating video, start in the real classroom, drift into the daydream, and come back to the real lesson. The fantasy is the hook; the real story is the payoff.
  • Move early. Before long, AI fantasy sequences will be everywhere. Most schools plan marketing a year at a time, and consent and approvals take time, so the schools that start now have a window of a cycle or two before it becomes the new generic.

A simple test before you use AI

Before any AI touches an image, ask three questions:

01

Is a real child’s likeness being generated or altered?

02

Would a parent feel misled if they knew how it was made?

03

If it needs a label, would the label hurt the message?

If the answer to any of them is yes, stop and think again.

Generally safe
  • Cleanup and retouching
  • Reformatting for different placements
  • Removing students without consent
  • Archive restoration, planning visuals, library management
Think twice
  • Motion added to scenery or buildings
  • A real student placed in an openly imaginative world
  • Labeled renders of facilities not yet built
Avoid
  • Animating identifiable students without specific consent
  • AI-generated “students” who don’t exist
  • Replacing real faces with generated ones

The fear, answered

Do I still worry that AI could reproduce my work? Less than I did. If it can’t imitate the look today, it probably will one day. But a look was never what schools were paying for. They were paying for proof: real children, at a real school, in moments that actually happened. AI can imitate a style. It can’t attend your school.

That’s the irony. The more convincing AI becomes, the more valuable the real thing becomes. Use AI to clear the cables, plan the shoot and dream up the trip to Mars. But when a parent looks at your website and asks, “Is this really what it’s like?”, make sure the answer is yes.
