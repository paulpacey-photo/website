01

Emphasis

Two schools may use identical words, but they rarely assign them identical weight. One leads with academic rigor; another opens with belonging and lets examination results follow. The vocabulary is shared; the hierarchy is not.

That makes emphasis a choice, starting with one simple question: distilled down to its core, what qualities should define your school above all else? A school that tries to be known for seven things is remembered for none. Three deliberate choices, given true weight, say far more than twenty words given equal billing.

02

Tangible distinctions

Generic claims like “academic excellence” can be pasted onto any competitor’s website. True differentiation relies on non-transferable facts: a unique history, exclusive accreditation milestones, or a distinct founding mission. Because these irreplaceable markers belong to one institution alone, anchoring a school’s identity in what is exclusively theirs builds a narrative grounded in undeniable reality rather than interchangeable adjectives.

Facilities provide that same competitive edge when treated as living evidence. A signature performing arts center or specialized laboratory cannot be easily duplicated by rivals. Shown in active use, these physical assets become visible proof of core values, offering parents immediate evidence of what sets the school apart.

03

Trajectory

When parents enroll a child, they aren’t just buying who a school is today; they are investing in where it is going. Most school websites broadcast current achievements, but forward-looking parents choose a school for the next decade. That makes a school’s trajectory one of its most powerful competitive differentiators.

Openly sharing active initiatives, evolving partnerships, and campus developments shows a school in motion. A visible, ambitious direction proves that claims of excellence and innovation aren’t just legacy slogans. It offers parents something far more compelling than present accomplishments: authentic proof that the school will be even better tomorrow.

04

Proof

The final place distinctiveness lives is proof—the tangible reality of how your school looks, acts, and feels in motion. In practice, this comes down to honest imagery.

If every school claims to foster curiosity and belonging, words alone lose power. Claims can invite skepticism, but unscripted visual proof earns trust. Parents aren’t easily fooled by clever messaging. They are looking for a consistent pattern of authenticity—a canvas of micro-moments that lets them confidently picture their child thriving in your care over the long haul.

That makes photography less of a marketing tool and more of an institutional stress-test. When what a school says and what it does line up, the images carry a conviction no copy can manufacture. When they don’t, when the language is warm but the interactions are guarded, or the words celebrate curiosity but the classrooms are silent rows, the camera tends to notice that too.

Show, don’t tell

There is a piece of advice so old it has become a cliché: show, don’t tell. Writers hear it in their first workshop and roll their eyes at it for years afterwards. But a cliché is usually a truth that has been repeated until people stopped hearing it, and I’ve come to believe this one may be the most important idea in school marketing today.

I was reminded of it recently while researching a group of schools in Italy. What struck me was how little they say up front. There is no wall of adjectives, no opening paragraph assuring me that the community is caring and the curriculum rigorous. The images carry the first impression, and it becomes clear very quickly what makes these schools different. They barely have to say it.

That is close to the opposite of where most of the category has settled: leading with the shared words, and letting the pictures follow as illustration.

Which brings me back to the three things you want your school to be known for, and to the hardest question I know how to ask a school:

“
If you removed every word from your website, would a parent looking only at what you show arrive at the same three things?

It is the same subtraction this article began with, taken one step further. Removing the superlatives revealed a vocabulary every school shares. Removing the words altogether reveals whether there is anything underneath that vocabulary a parent can actually see.

If the answer is no, the words are making promises the school hasn’t backed up. They become what every overused tagline becomes: hollow and forgettable, because nothing in front of the parent gives them anything to hold on to.

If the answer is yes, your school has done something most schools haven’t. It has proved its claims rather than simply made them. The words become captions for evidence the parent has already seen, and that is the only position from which words like “caring” and “rigorous” persuade anyone.

What this means for schools

If your positioning rests on an eloquently crafted mission statement, you are competing on the one ground where everyone has already arrived at the same place. The more productive work lies elsewhere: choosing the three things you want to be known for and giving them the weight they deserve; letting the facts that are true of no one else do the proving, wherever they support those three things; showing parents where the school is heading, not only where it stands; and making sure that what you show proves what you say.

That last one is the hardest, and the most valuable. It can’t be settled in a brand workshop. It can only be settled by looking.

One finding deserves a harder look. Every one of the 100 schools described itself as a community. If community is the heart of your positioning, it’s worth asking honestly whether it is setting you apart at all. It may well be true, and it may be what your families value most. But on its own, it is the one claim every competitor makes too. Unless you can show it more convincingly than every school a parent is comparing you with, it may be time to rethink how you stand out.

After all my years behind the camera, the most useful thing I’ve learned about international school marketing is that the schools that stand out aren’t always the ones with the best mission statement. They are the ones whose words turn out to be true when you look.

So the next time you look at your website, your prospectus or your social media feed, cover the words and ask yourself the question. Would a parent, looking only at what you show, arrive at the same three things?

About the study

In November 2026, working with Claude (Anthropic’s AI assistant), I analyzed the websites of 100 international schools and school groups: 40 in Europe, 20 in the Middle East, 20 in Asia, 12 in the Americas and 8 in Africa. That came to 288 pages in all, covering each homepage and the pages where schools describe their mission, values and character. Every piece of text was measured by how often it appears and how large it is on screen. Word variants were counted together (for example, inspire, inspiring and inspired), and generic words such as school, students and curriculum were left out. Eight schools whose websites could not be read were replaced with comparable schools from the same region. The study repeats a first study of 57 school websites from October 2026, which found the same 20 words. No school is named, because the point is the pattern, not any individual school.
