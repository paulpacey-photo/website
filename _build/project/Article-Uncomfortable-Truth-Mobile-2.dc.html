This is not a failure

The easy response to this finding is cynicism: schools are lazy, marketing is empty, it’s all the same brochure. I want to resist that, because I think it’s wrong, and because I’ve worked with enough school marketing teams to know how thoughtful and hardworking they generally are.

What I found isn’t a failure of marketing. It’s what marketing looks like in a mature category that has been optimized to resonate with prospective families.

When a market becomes sophisticated, when every serious participant understands its audience well, the messaging converges. Everyone learns what the discerning customer values, and everyone tells the truth about offering it. International school parents want their children to be safe, known and cared for. They want academic challenge. They want a genuinely international outlook. They want an environment where difference is welcomed rather than tolerated. Good schools provide these things, so good schools say so. And because most good schools really are more alike than different, the words they choose are bound to overlap.

The words are shared because the values are shared. And the values are shared because they’re right.

This is the tricky part. Not that schools are saying false things, but that they are all saying true things, the same true things, to the same careful parent, who is reading five or six of these websites in a single evening and finding them blurring together.

So the challenge isn’t that schools sound the same. It’s that words, however well chosen, can only make a claim. Proving it takes something else.

The implication is quiet but significant: positioning no longer lives in the words we use. A school cannot out-word its competitors, because there are no better words left. “Caring” has no more distinctive synonym. Refining the adjectives, commissioning another round of brand language, debating whether “nurturing” or “supportive” sounds warmer: this work has a very low ceiling. It can make a school’s language clearer. It cannot make it different.

So where does the difference go?

Four places distinctiveness lives

Looking closely at the data and the schools behind it, genuine differentiation survives in four distinct places. None of them is in the dictionary.

01

Emphasis

Two schools may use identical words, but they rarely assign them identical weight. One leads with academic rigor; another opens with belonging and lets examination results follow. The vocabulary is shared; the hierarchy is not.

That makes emphasis a choice, starting with one simple question: distilled down to its core, what qualities should define your school above all else? A school that tries to be known for seven things is remembered for none. Three deliberate choices, given true weight, say far more than twenty words given equal billing.

02

Tangible distinctions

Generic claims like “academic excellence” can be pasted onto any competitor’s website. True differentiation relies on non-transferable facts: a unique history, exclusive accreditation milestones, or a distinct founding mission. Because they belong to one school alone, a story built on them rests on fact, not on adjectives anyone could use.

Facilities can do the same job when they’re shown as living evidence. A signature performing arts center or a specialized laboratory is hard for a competitor to copy. In active use, they show parents at a glance what the school values and what sets it apart.

03

Trajectory

When parents enroll a child, they aren’t just buying who a school is today; they are investing in where it is going. Most school websites broadcast current achievements, but forward-looking parents choose a school for the next decade. That makes where a school is heading one of the clearest ways it can stand apart.

Openly sharing active initiatives, evolving partnerships, and campus developments shows a school in motion. A clear direction shows that words like “innovative” are more than old slogans. It gives parents something more compelling than a list of achievements: a reason to believe the school will be better in five years than it is today.

04

Proof

The final place distinctiveness lives is proof—the tangible reality of how your school looks, acts, and feels in motion, from your hero imagery to your social feed. In practice, this comes down to consistent, honest imagery.

If every school claims to foster curiosity and belonging, words alone lose power. Claims can invite skepticism, but unscripted visual proof earns trust. Parents aren’t easily fooled by clever messaging. They are looking for a consistent pattern of real moments, enough of them to let them confidently picture their child thriving in your care over the long haul. A teacher leaning in the second a student finally understands. Two children laughing at a joke neither could translate.

That makes photography less of a marketing tool and more of an institutional stress-test. When what a school says and what it does line up, the images carry a conviction no copy can manufacture. When they don’t, when the language is warm but the interactions are guarded, or the words celebrate curiosity but the classrooms are silent rows, the camera tends to notice that too.

Photography shouldn’t merely be decoration for a brand, but evidence for it.
