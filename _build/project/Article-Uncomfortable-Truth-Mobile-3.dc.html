What this means for schools

If your positioning rests on an eloquently crafted mission statement, you are competing on the one ground where everyone has already arrived at the same place. The more productive work lies elsewhere: choosing the three things you want to be known for and giving them the weight they deserve; letting the facts that are true of no one else do the proving, wherever they support those three things; and making sure that what you show proves what you say.

That last one is the hardest, and the most valuable. It can’t be settled in a brand workshop. It can only be settled by looking.

One finding deserves a harder look. Every one of the 100 schools described itself as a community. If community is the heart of your positioning, it’s worth asking honestly whether it is setting you apart at all. It may well be true, and it may be what your families value most. But on its own, it is the one claim every competitor makes too. Unless you can show it more convincingly than every school a parent is comparing you with, it may be time to rethink how you stand out.

After all my years behind the camera, the most useful thing I’ve learned about international school marketing is that the schools that stand out aren’t always the ones with the best mission statement. They are the ones whose words turn out to be true when you look.

So the next time you look at your website, your prospectus or your social media feed, cover the words and ask yourself the question. Would a parent, looking only at what you show, arrive at the same three things?

About the study

In November 2026, working with Claude (Anthropic’s AI assistant), I analyzed the websites of 100 international schools and school groups: 40 in Europe, 20 in the Middle East, 20 in Asia, 12 in the Americas and 8 in Africa. That came to 288 pages in all, covering each homepage and the pages where schools describe their mission, values and character. Every piece of text was measured by how often it appears and how large it is on screen. Word variants were counted together (for example, inspire, inspiring and inspired), and generic words such as school, students and curriculum were left out. Eight schools whose websites could not be read were replaced with comparable schools from the same region. The study repeats a first study of 57 school websites from October 2026, which found the same 20 words. No school is named, because the point is the pattern, not any individual school.
