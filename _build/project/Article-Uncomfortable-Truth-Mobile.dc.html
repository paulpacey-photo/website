The Uncomfortable Truth About International School Marketing

What remains when you strip the carefully worded mission statements away, and where a school’s real distinctiveness has been living all along.

Paul PaceyPhotographer to international schools · Prague
Cover image · placeholder

I’ve spent more than a decade on the creative front line of international schools. I’ve been in their corridors before the first bell, in their science labs and music rooms, on their playing fields in the last light of the afternoon. Through more campaigns and countries than I can count, I have developed a strong instinct about what makes one school feel different from another, and very little of it has to do with what schools say about themselves.

I’ve always been able to feel those differences, but never able to objectively quantify them. Until recently.

An experiment in subtraction

For some time now, I’ve been developing software designed to read a school’s website in a way that consolidates and prioritizes its positioning pillars into actionable words that would translate visually. Think of it as both a marketing audit and an action plan, used to better serve my clients.

Beyond analyzing visual content (an article for another day), the software crawls a site and scores its language by prominence, measuring word frequency, visual scale and page placement. Then it does what a prospective parent rarely has time to do: it strips away the superlative, ‘fluffy’ vocabulary to reveal what lies beneath.

By fluffy, I mean the words every institution reaches for when it wants to sound impressive: exceptional, world-class, outstanding, excellent, limitless. These words aren’t lies. Most of the schools using them are, in fact, very good. But they carry no real information. A parent can’t learn anything about a school from being told it is exceptional, because every school they are considering says the same.

Once those words are gone, what remains should be the school’s real self-description, the words it has chosen to define its true character.

I ran the tool on five schools with no connection to each other. Different cities, different ownership, different histories, catering to different families walking through the gates each morning.

The vast majority of what each school presented as its distinctive character appeared, in nearly the same words, on the others’ sites. If you’d swapped their vocabularies overnight, I doubt any of their communities would have noticed.

School after school, the pattern held everywhere I looked.

This is not a failure

The easy response to this finding is cynicism: schools are lazy, marketing is empty, it’s all the same brochure. I want to resist that, because I think it’s wrong, and because I’ve worked with enough school marketing teams to know how thoughtful and hardworking they generally are.

What I found isn’t a failure of marketing. It’s what marketing looks like in a mature category that has been optimized to resonate with prospective families.

When a market becomes sophisticated, when every serious participant understands its audience well, the messaging converges. Everyone learns what the discerning customer values, and everyone tells the truth about offering it. International school parents want their children to be safe, known and cared for. They want academic challenge. They want a genuinely international outlook. They want an environment where difference is welcomed rather than tolerated. Good schools provide these things, so good schools say so.
