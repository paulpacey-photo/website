AI in International School Marketing: The Good, the Bad and the Ugly

Why schools are right to be wary of AI, why they’re wrong to give up on it, and how to tell the difference.

Paul PaceyPhotographer to international schools · Prague

When I first started using AI in my editing, it felt like a gift. Cables snaking across a classroom floor, a trash bin in the corner of an otherwise perfect frame, a horizontal image that needed to work as a vertical story: problems that once cost me hours disappeared in seconds.

Then I discovered video generation, and I became euphoric at the possibilities. A school could hire me for a photo shoot and walk away with a polished, full-scale video campaign—built from those same images, in the exact same aesthetic, for a fraction of the time and cost. Combined with my editing and sound design skills, I could become a true one-stop shop, delivering a product that rivaled—or surpassed—what dedicated video production companies could offer.

Then came the fear: If a school could train an AI on my style—a visual signature I’ve spent 15 years refining—why would they ever need to hire me again?

Today, I’m seeing something I didn’t expect: schools are stepping back from AI content altogether. Part of me finds that reassuring. But another part is more than a little disappointed. In their rush to mitigate risk, it feels like schools might be throwing the baby out with the bathwater.

Perhaps AI is neither the existential threat nor the miracle cure it first appeared to be. Used carelessly, it undermines the exact authenticity school marketing exists to build. Used intentionally, it still offers real magic. This article is about telling the difference.

The good: what AI already does well

Start with the unglamorous wins, because they’re real.

  • Cleaning up the frame. Cables, bins, parked cars and outdated signage, gone in seconds.
  • Reformatting. Extending a background so a horizontal image works as a vertical story or a wide web banner.
  • Protecting privacy. Removing or obscuring a student in the background whose family hasn’t given consent. Here, AI solves a consent problem instead of creating one.
  • Restoring the archive. Bringing faded photographs back to life for an anniversary or a heritage page.
  • Planning. Mocking up a concept so a leadership team can approve an idea before a single frame is shot.
  • Managing the library. Tagging, captions and alt text that make hundreds of images searchable by theme, season or year group.
  • Showing what doesn’t exist yet. A clearly labeled “artist’s impression” of a new building.
  • *Bringing images to life. Turning still photographs into short, moving moments.

This last point deserves an asterisk, because it is precisely where the slope starts to get slippery. In its current form, AI-generated motion is still hit-or-miss, but when it lands, it is almost impossible to distinguish from reality. In my world, I’ve learned this represents both the greatest opportunity and the greatest threat of our time.

My first laborious attempt to harness this technology produced a two-and-a-half-minute demo reel that I remain proud of to this day. Set to music, and artfully sequenced in a poetic story arc, it captured something genuine beyond what any single image could possibly accomplish. I will stand before any critic and defend the fact that I created nothing that wasn’t already present in the original stills. Emotionally and aesthetically, the reel was simply an extension and amplification of the core principles my work has always stood for.

But just as I thought I had found the keys to the kingdom, I had a rude awakening.

The bad: the line I didn’t see

The moment I understood what was really at stake came from a client I’d worked with for more than a decade. I’d spent days on a sample reel that brought some of my favorite images from their shoot to life, and I was proud of it. I still am. I sent it expecting delight.

What came back was alarm, and they were right to raise it. I had been thinking like a creative. They were thinking like guardians. Those weren’t just my images; they were their students, now moving in ways no parent had agreed to, in a video the school hadn’t asked for. However beautiful the result, I had stepped over a line I hadn’t known was there.

That’s the part of AI that rarely makes the headlines. The technology was never the problem. Consent was.

What the rules now say

The law has caught up faster than most of us expected. Here’s where things stand as of October 2026. It’s a general overview, not legal advice, so check anything specific with your data protection officer or legal team.

  • Labeling is now mandatory in the EU. Since August 2, 2026, the EU AI Act has required anyone who publishes a “deepfake” to disclose that it was generated or manipulated by AI (Legalithm).
  • “Deepfake” means more than you think. It isn’t limited to face swaps. It covers realistic AI-made or AI-altered images, audio or video that resemble real people, places or events and could pass as authentic (Davis+Gilbert). Animating a real photograph of a real student falls squarely inside it.
  • Advertising gets little relief. There’s a lighter regime for evidently artistic works, but the European Commission’s guidelines treat it as very narrow for advertising (Bird & Bird).
  • Your photo consent probably doesn’t cover it. Most consent forms cover photographing students and using the images in marketing. Animating a child’s likeness with AI is a different use, and it needs consent that names it. Data protection authorities around the world have made clear that realistic AI images of identifiable people, especially children, are personal data (GRC Report).
  • Enforcement is national and complaint-driven. Each EU country enforces the labeling rules, with fines of up to €15 million or 3% of turnover (Responsible AI Platform). Early enforcement has been slow, but anyone can complain: a parent, a competitor, a journalist.
  • Outside the EU, details differ. Switzerland has decided against a specific deepfake law (Swissinfo), and the UK isn’t covered by the EU Act. Data protection rules on children’s images apply everywhere.

The label problem

Which brings me back to my reel. I’d stand by it today. Because I made it before the new rules took effect, it doesn’t legally need a label. But if I made the same reel now, it would, and I know how that label would be read: fake. To me, the reel shows real children, real feeling and real moments, only in motion. A label tells you how something was made, not whether it’s true to what happened.
