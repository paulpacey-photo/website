The Uncomfortable Truth About International School Marketing

What remains when you strip the carefully worded mission statements away, and what actually persuades a parent to choose one good school over another.

Paul PaceyPhotographer to international schools · Prague

I’ve spent more than a decade on the creative front line of international schools. I’ve been in their corridors before the first bell, in their science labs and music rooms, on their playing fields in the last light of the afternoon. Through more campaigns and countries than I can count, I’ve come to an uncomfortable truth that most school marketing quietly avoids: good international schools have far more in common than they would have you think.

What makes the battle for differentiation even harder is that when you take a closer look at how most international schools position themselves, you would probably find that you could swap one school’s mission statement or brand pyramid for a competitor’s overnight, and nobody would even notice.

I decided to put this to the test.

An experiment in subtraction

I used Claude to gather the websites of 57 international schools and school groups with no connection to each other: Europe, the Middle East, Asia, Africa and the Americas; IB, British and American curricula; small independents and large groups. It analyzed the words each school uses to describe who it is (mission, values, “why choose us”) the way a busy parent takes them in, paying attention not just to how often a word appears, but to how prominently it’s used.

Next came the subtraction. We set aside the superlatives, the words every institution reaches for when it wants to sound impressive: excellent, world-class, outstanding, exceptional, leading. These words aren’t lies. Most of the schools using them are, in fact, very good. But they carry no real information. A parent can’t learn anything about a school from being told it is exceptional, because every school they are considering says the same. And nearly every one does: 56 of the 57 sites used at least one superlative, and “excellence” alone appeared on 49.

Once the superlatives are gone, in theory, what remains should be each school’s real self-description, the words it has chosen to define its true character. If schools were as different as their marketing suggests, surely this is where it would show.

What remained

It didn’t. Site after site, what remained was the same predictable list of buzzwords and so-called differentiators. As many school marketers already suspect, the result wasn’t a surprise—it was proof.

Out of 57 sites, 54 described themselves as a community. 51 called themselves supportive, and 51 creative. 49 promised to inspire.

The average site used 15 of these 20 words. Even the most distinctive used five. And when I looked only at the ten words each school gave the most visual weight, an average of six or seven came from this same shared list.
