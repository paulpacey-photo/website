Scheduling guide

“Just Work Your Magic”
Isn’t a Plan

The do’s and don’ts of a winning shoot schedule.

Read the guide ↓ Printable PDF

“All the teachers know you’re here. Feel free to just walk around and work your magic.”

It’s meant as a vote of confidence, and it usually comes from a busy marketing team with the best of intentions. But after more than 200 school campaigns, I can tell you it’s one of the most expensive sentences in school photography. It sounds like freedom. In practice, it means the best moments of the day are happening in rooms nobody thought to check.

There are three ways to approach a shoot day: stage it, leave it to chance, or cultivate it. Only one of them gives you images that are both authentic and on purpose. This guide is about that one, and it starts with the schedule.

Three approaches

Three approaches. You be the judge.

Most schools approach a shoot day in one of three ways. Each has its logic, and each has a price.

Staged

“Okay, so just point at the beakers and pretend you like science.”

Staging feels safe. You decide the shot, set it up and get it. When you need something very specific, like a facility shot or a particular moment for a prospectus spread, a degree of direction is useful. The problem is what it does to people. Students stiffen up, expressions get exaggerated, and props appear that don’t belong. Parents may not be able to say why an image feels off, but they feel it, and a picture that looks manufactured quietly undermines everything else you say about your school.

Pros
Predictable, precise, quick to tick off a list.
Cons
Looks performed, costs credibility, and rarely moves anyone.

Unplanned

“All the teachers know you’re here. Feel free to just walk around and work your magic.”

The unplanned approach aims for authenticity, and it’s easy on everyone: no meetings, no disruption, just a photographer wandering the halls. Occasionally, it works. More often, you arrive just after the moment, find a class doing silent reading, or discover that “all the teachers know” meant an email many of them never opened. Authenticity without purpose leaves gaps, and you only find them when the images come back.

Pros
Genuine moments, little preparation, no disruption to lessons.
Cons
Relies on luck, wastes time, and leaves the images you actually needed to chance.

Cultivated

“Let’s get the specimens on the tables, pair up the students and let them start exploring.”

Cultivating sits between the two. You can’t schedule spontaneity, but you can schedule the conditions for it: the right class, the right activity, the right teacher, at the right time. Then you step back and let it unfold. What you give up in control, you make up for in moments that can’t be faked. The honest cost is time up front: conversations with teachers, a bit of back and forth, and a schedule built with care rather than copied from the timetable.

Pros
Authentic and on purpose, efficient on the day, teachers invested in the result.
Cons
Takes more planning, and depends on teacher buy-in.

The rest of this guide is about making that planning count.

The plan

Planning a shoot doesn’t mean deciding what every photo will look like. That’s staging by another name. It means deciding where and when the good moments are most likely to happen, so the photographer is already in the room when they do.

Over the years, one pattern has held remarkably true: the number of real, specific opportunities you can identify before the shoot is the single best predictor of how the day will go. A plan doesn’t kill spontaneity. It gives spontaneity somewhere to happen.

Do.

Plan the opportunities, not the pictures

Planning is what makes spontaneity possible.

  • Decide where and when the moments will happen
  • Then step back and let them unfold naturally
Don’t.

Don’t “just walk around and work your magic”

Without a plan, the best moments happen where you aren’t.

  • Wandering looks like the authentic option, but it wastes the day
  • You arrive after the moment, not before it
  • Spontaneity still needs a plan to happen in front of the camera

Who picks the sessions

The most common shortcut is to hand the schedule to a department head, who looks at the timetable and ticks the lessons that sound good on paper. It feels organized. In practice, it can be worse than wandering. Once a session is on the schedule, the photographer is committed to it, even when the class turns out to be a quiet worksheet. By the time that’s clear, the better moment down the corridor has already come and gone.

The people who know which lessons will come alive are the teachers running them. Give them a short menu of activity options for each goal and age group, and let them choose what fits. Start with what’s already happening. It’s the least disruptive approach, and often the most genuine.

Do.

Offer choices, not orders

A teacher who chose the activity will make it work.

  • A menu of activity options for each goal and age group
  • Teachers pick what fits their timetable
  • Start with what’s already happening: least disruptive, often most genuine
Don’t.

Don’t let the timetable build the schedule

What looks good on paper rarely tells you what will happen in the room.

  • Sessions get picked from the timetable, not from what’s really planned
  • Teachers may or may not know the photographer is coming
  • You’re committed to a class that won’t pay off, while the better moment happens elsewhere
  • It’s almost worse than walking around and hoping

The conversation

“A photographer is coming on Thursday” is a heads-up, not a brief. What you mean is “plan something engaging we can capture.” What many teachers hear is “carry on as normal,” or worse, “put on a show.” Neither gets you the images you need.

A five-minute conversation in person does what a chain of emails can’t. You get answers faster, you learn more about what’s really planned, and you can tell which teachers are open to adapting a lesson for twenty minutes to try something they hadn’t planned. Those teachers are worth their weight in gold.

Do.

Build the schedule with teachers, face to face

A timetable shows what’s happening on paper, not what will happen in the room.

  • Talking in person is faster and more informed than waiting on emails
  • You see how open each teacher is to adapting a lesson for 20 minutes
  • You find the teachers who will make a moment come alive
Don’t.

Don’t assume “a photographer is coming” means “make it exciting”

What you mean and what a teacher hears are rarely the same.

  • Most teachers will simply carry on as normal
  • Some will put on something stiff for the camera
  • A real conversation is the only brief that works

The brief

The most common mistake schools make is casting too wide a net: a long list of every subject, every year group and every space, all in one day. Every extra item takes time from the others, and the whole set comes back thinner for it.

Start with what your website, brochures and campaigns actually need, then measure the schedule against it. Once teachers have responded, look for the gaps: the goal nobody picked up, the age group that’s missing. Close them with a few select teachers before the day, not during it.

Do.

Check the plan against the goals

Close the gaps before the day, not during it.

  • Compare teachers’ picks with your priorities
  • Invite select teachers to fill whatever is missing
Don’t.

Don’t ask for everything

The longer the wish list, the thinner each shot gets.

  • A wide net spreads shoot time too thin
  • Start from what your website, brochures and campaigns actually need
  • Fewer, better opportunities beat a long checklist

5 of 5

The day

A realistic shoot day holds somewhere between 13 and 18 opportunities, at 20 to 30 minutes each. Early Years and PE need a full period, because the youngest students need time to forget the camera is there, and sport needs time to build. Squeezing in one more session always feels efficient, but it takes quality from every slot around it.

Clear the admin before the day so the day can be about the moments: confirm every teacher, notify parents and update the non-media list. Keep a list of backup teachers in case a session falls through. And assign a school liaison, someone who knows the building and the people, keeps the schedule moving, and covers safeguarding while the photographer focuses on the moments.

Do.

Clear the admin early, and don’t shoot alone

On the day, the focus belongs on the moments, not the paperwork.

  • Confirm every teacher ahead of time
  • Notify parents and update the non-media list
  • Assign a school liaison to guide, keep things moving and cover safeguarding
Don’t.

Don’t cram the day

One more session feels efficient, but it costs quality everywhere.

  • Allow 20–30 minutes per opportunity; 13–18 make a full day
  • Give Early Years and PE a full period
  • Keep backup teachers ready in case something falls through

None of this takes a bigger budget. It takes a better plan, made with the right people, a little earlier than feels necessary. Get the schedule right and the day takes care of itself.
